What a cruise-ship speed test actually tells you

A speed test on a cruise is one device, on a shared link, for a few seconds. It is not the speed the cruise line promised you, because most lines do not publish a per-cabin megabit guarantee. Use the test to see whether your package is working. Do not treat a single number as the ship’s internet.

Why the number moves

Passenger internet on modern ships is mostly satellite capacity shared by guests and crew. Starlink’s case study for Carnival Corporation describes its own satellites as flying in low Earth orbit, about 550 km up, versus geostationary satellites about 36,000 km up, and says adding Starlink raised that company’s throughput and cut latency compared with the older setup. Those figures are the vendor’s report about fleet capacity. They are not a speed you will read on your phone.

The same limit shows up in how lines talk about their product. Royal Caribbean markets VOOM as available on every Royal Caribbean ship and describes it as up to six times faster than Wi-Fi on other cruise ships. That “up to” is Royal Caribbean’s comparison, not an independent test of your cabin at 5 p.m.

A Starlink case study about Royal Caribbean Group says the whole fleet was equipped and describes a peak symmetrical throughput of 10 Gbps on Star of the Seas after a 2025 gateway installation. That number is ship uplink, not megabits per passenger. Thousands of phones share it. Your test falls when many of them upload at once.

How to test without fooling yourself

  • Test twice. Once when the ship is quiet, once in the evening when people stream. A single test cannot describe the day.
  • Read down and up. Photo backups and video calls need the upload, not only the download number.
  • Stand where you will work. A test in the atrium does not describe the balcony or the cabin if the Wi-Fi does not reach there. Norwegian’s connectivity lead has said publicly that losing the signal on a balcony is a complaint they treat as unacceptable, which tells you coverage is a separate problem from satellite speed.
  • Match the package to the job. Buy the tier the line says includes streaming or video calls only if you will do those things. Email and messaging do not need the top tier on every line. The price is on that sailing’s internet page.

What not to believe

Do not believe a blog that quotes “15 Mbps and 800 ms” as if it measured your ship. This one will not. Do not believe “six times faster” without asking six times faster than what, on which ship, at what hour. And do not assume your phone’s normal roaming works in the same way as ship Wi-Fi. Maritime cellular is a different product with its own rates. Read your carrier’s cruise or maritime policy before you leave the handset in roaming-on.

If your job needs an unbroken video call, pick the line and the package that currently say they support it, then test on embarkation afternoon while you can still change plans. Weather, the ship’s location, and how many people are online still apply.

Keep a fallback that does not need the ship’s Wi-Fi: messages already downloaded, boarding documents saved offline, and a plan for the port day if a call cannot wait. Ship internet is for the hours you are out of cellular range. In port, the pier network or a local SIM can be a different connection entirely, with its own price. Do not assume the package you bought for sea days is the cheapest way to upload photos while the ship is docked.
