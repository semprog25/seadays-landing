What a drink package actually includes

A cruise drink package is a prepaid beverage plan sold by the cruise line—not a single product across the industry. Most mainstream “deluxe” or “premium” plans cover a defined list of drinks up to a per-drink price cap. Soda-only, non-alcoholic, beer-and-wine, and coffee bundles are separate SKUs with different rules.

Before you compare prices, open the current inclusions list for your sailing. Packages commonly cover draft beer, house wine, and standard cocktails up to a stated limit. They often exclude or cap specialty items: premium spirits above the cap, some bottled wines, fresh-squeezed juice, energy drinks, souvenir glasses, mini-bar stock, room-service beverages, and drinks in some specialty venues. Bottled water brands vary by line—some plans include select brands, others do not.

“Unlimited” still means unlimited within that list and cap, not every drink on the ship. If your habit is dinner wine by the bottle or a specific premium spirit, check whether the package applies at all before you treat it as full-bar access.

Gratuities and other hidden charges

Published package prices may or may not include the service charge the line adds to beverage sales. Some fares show an all-in package total; others add a daily or per-drink gratuity on top of the sticker price. Compare like with like: if your à la carte estimate includes auto-gratuity on each drink, your package comparison must use the same all-in package figure from your invoice.

Other costs sit outside the package even when you buy one: specialty dining wine pairings, corkage if you bring your own bottle where allowed, upgraded pours above the package cap, and anything bought ashore in port. A package does not refund unused days; if you pay for seven days but effectively drink on five, that is a planning cost, not a line error.

Who usually benefits—and who does not

Packages tend to help guests who will order several covered drinks on most sea days and many evenings in port, using items that are actually on the inclusion list. They help less when you mostly drink included dining-room coffee, iced tea, and tap water, or when you spend long hours ashore and drink on land instead.

Embarkation afternoon and disembarkation morning count as full days on many plans even when you are not onboard all day. Light drinkers, occasional wine-at-dinner guests, and anyone who rarely orders from the covered list usually do better paying as they go—especially when they are only one drink away from “break-even” on paper.

Household, age, and line-policy rules

Cabin rules matter as much as menu math. On many lines, every adult in the stateroom must purchase the same alcoholic package if anyone buys it—you cannot mix one unlimited plan with three pay-as-you-go adults. Some lines allow different tiers; some require matching plans for the whole cabin. Read the rule for your booking before you assume one heavy drinker can carry the household.

Alcoholic packages are limited to guests who meet the line’s drinking age for that itinerary (often 21 on ships with heavy US port calls; 18 on some other routes). Teen or child soda packages are not interchangeable with adult plans. Promotions tied to fare type (drink package “included” in a fare) may already cover part of what you would buy—do not stack assumptions without checking the fare grid.

Policies change by brand and sometimes by ship class. Virgin Voyages, for example, often frames bar spend differently from Carnival-style unlimited packages. Treat line-specific behavior as something to verify on your contract, not as a universal rule in a blog post.

Checklist before you buy

  1. Write down what you actually drank on your last vacation—not what you plan to drink at sea.
  2. Download or screenshot the package inclusion PDF for your ship and sailing dates.
  3. Note whether gratuity is included in the package price on your invoice.
  4. Confirm cabin-wide purchase rules and drinking-age limits for your travelers.
  5. Count port days vs sea days; heavy ashore time usually lowers onboard consumption (see when packages lose money on port-heavy trips).
  6. If you are still interested, run numbers with your real menu prices—walkthrough in SeaDays drink package calculator math or the drink package calculator.
  7. If you are within a drink or two of break-even, default to pay-as-you-go; unused prepaid drinks are the expensive outcome.
