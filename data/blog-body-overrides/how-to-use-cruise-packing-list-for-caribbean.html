How to use a packing list for a Caribbean cruise

A Caribbean cruise packing list fails when it is copied from an Alaska or fjord trip. You need sun protection, insect protection for some ports, enough medicine for the whole sailing, and a way to charge devices that your cruise line actually allows. You do not need parkas.

Build the list from the sailing, not from a generic “cruise capsule.” Use the itinerary’s ports, the line’s prohibited-items page, and a health kit sized to the number of days plus a delay.

Start with the health kit

The CDC’s cruise ship travel page tells passengers to pack prescriptions and over-the-counter medicines for the entire trip, plus extra in case of delays. Add sunscreen labeled SPF 15 or higher, alcohol-based hand sanitizer, and insect repellent if the ports have mosquitoes or ticks. The same page says sunburn happens on cloudy days, and that bugs in some ports can carry dengue, Zika, chikungunya, malaria, or yellow fever. Check the specific ports on a CDC destination page rather than assuming every Caribbean call has the same risk.

If you get seasick, talk to a clinician before you sail. CDC notes that some common medicines, including certain antidepressants, painkillers, and birth-control pills, can make motion sickness worse. Bring the medicine you were advised to use. Do not plan to diagnose yourself from a blog.

If you are sick before embarkation, CDC says not to travel and to ask the line about rescheduling. During the cruise, report symptoms to the medical center.

Clothes for heat, water, and one nicer dinner

  • Daytime ashore: light clothing you can walk in, a hat, and shoes that survive a wet pier or a tender. Closed toes beat new sandals on uneven docks.
  • Swim and after-swim: a swimsuit, a cover-up for indoor halls, and a small towel only if your line does not lend them on the pool deck. Check the ship.
  • Evening: one outfit that meets the line’s dining dress code for that ship. Caribbean heat does not cancel a “cruise elegant” night if the daily program lists one.
  • Rain: a compact rain layer. Afternoon storms are common in the region; a down jacket is not the tool.

Reef-safe claims on sunscreen labels are a product statement, not a port law. If a specific island bans certain chemicals, the island’s own notice is the rule.

Power: do not pack a banned strip

Royal Caribbean’s prohibited-items policy forbids extension cords, power strips, and multi-plug outlets. The allowed exception it publishes is a device or USB charger that carries a recognized U.S. or European conformity mark. Other lines write their own rules. Read the prohibited list for the line on your ticket. A packing blog that says “bring a power strip because cabins have few outlets” can get the strip taken at the gangway.

Also check your airline’s lithium-battery rule before you put a power bank in checked luggage. Many airlines require power banks in the cabin.

How to turn this into a shared list

Split the list into three bags of responsibility: documents and medicines (one adult), sun and insect kit (whoever is ashore most), and evening clothes (each person). Mark items the ship already provides, such as a hair dryer, only after you see them on that ship’s stateroom page. Duplicating the ship’s dryer is how Caribbean suitcases get heavy.

The night before embarkation, put passports, the boarding pass, prescriptions in original packaging if your line requires it, and the first day’s ashore shoes in the carry-on. The rest can live in the checked bag you will not see until the cabin is ready.
