Do Cruises Actually Harm the Environment? What the Data Really Says in 2026

Published by SeaDays Apr 1, 2026 Updated Apr 13, 2026 6 min read

What the shipping emissions data actually says

The widely quoted greenhouse-gas study for ships is about shipping, not a cruise-passenger footprint. The Fourth IMO GHG Study 2020, summarized on the International Maritime Organization’s site, estimates greenhouse-gas emissions from total shipping (international, domestic, and fishing) at 977 million tonnes in 2012 and 1,076 million tonnes in 2018. It puts shipping’s share of global anthropogenic emissions at 2.76% in 2012 and 2.89% in 2018. Those tonnes are not “cruises.” They include cargo ships.

The same study’s executive summary says container ships, cruise ships, and oil tankers have a smaller share of their own emissions in the phase it defines as cruising, because those ships spend more time slow or near port. That sentence is easy to misread. It does not say a cruise holiday is a small part of your personal emissions, and it does not publish a single percentage for “cruise ships cause X% of global CO₂.” If a page gives you that percentage without a table, it invented it.

What a passenger can check

  • The itinerary. Days at sea and days in port change how a ship runs. The IMO point about time near port is a fleet pattern, not a reason to skip or book a particular call.
  • The line’s own environmental report. Some lines publish fuel, shore-power, and wastewater figures for a year. Use that report’s year and scope. Do not mix a 2018 global inventory with a 2026 brochure.
  • Shore power. A ship plugged in at a pier is not burning the same way as a ship running generators. Whether your port offers it is a port and ship question. Look at the port authority or the line’s call sheet, not a generic “green cruise” label.

Emissions are not the sewage rules

The IMO greenhouse-gas study counts gases. It is not the rulebook for garbage or sewage. Those sit in MARPOL, the same organization’s pollution convention. Annex V is garbage. A page that quotes the 2.89% shipping share and then tells you whether your ship dumps waste is using the wrong document. For waste, start at the IMO’s MARPOL pages and then the cruise line’s environmental report for the year you are sailing.

Passenger behavior is the small part you control: use the bins the ship labels, do not throw anything over the side, and do not treat a “green” marketing line as a measurement. The measurement, when it exists, is in the line’s report or in the IMO inventory, with a year attached.

What this article will not claim

It will not rank cruise lines by cleanliness. It will not convert the IMO tonnes into “your week equals X flights.” That conversion needs assumptions about occupancy, fuel, and the flights you would have taken, and this page does not have those assumptions from a source that covers your sailing.

If you want a number for a decision, start with the IMO totals so you know the scale of all shipping, then read the cruise line’s latest environmental report for the fleet you are actually booking. Those are two different documents. Putting them in one sentence without saying so is how bad cruise-environment articles get written.

Check the date on whichever PDF you open. The Fourth GHG Study stops at 2018. A line’s report is usually a later year and a smaller fleet. Neither number updates itself because the article title says 2026.
